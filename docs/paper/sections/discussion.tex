\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Relationship Between the Two Approximations}

The two approximations have different theoretical foundations yet share the
same two anchor points: both are exact in the homogeneous case and have the
correct heavy-traffic singularity.

$\Tul$ works by confining the approximation within the known bounds.
Its interpolation weight $\alpha = (\rho_1+\rho_2)/8$ has a natural
interpretation: it is one-quarter of the average utilization, calibrated
to reproduce the Nelson--Tantawi formula at $r = 1$.
By construction, $0 \leq \alpha \leq 1/4$, so $\Tul$ always lies between
the bounds.

$\Tlh$ works from a different direction: it constructs a rational function
that matches the system's known asymptotic behavior at both traffic extremes.
The heavy-traffic factor $h(r) = 1 + \tfrac{3}{8}r^{-\beta}$ captures the
physical transition from a regime where both servers jointly saturate
($r = 1$, $h = 11/8$) to a regime where only the bottleneck server saturates
($r \to \infty$, $h \to 1$).
The sharpness of this transition---$h \approx 1$ already at $r = 1.5$---reflects
the fact that even modest heterogeneity is sufficient to break the joint
saturation and transfer the heavy-load correlation penalty to the bottleneck
alone.

Varma and Makowski~\citep{Varma1994} applied the Reiman--Simon framework to
the \emph{symmetric} ($\mu_1 = \mu_2$) fork-join queue with $n \geq 2$ servers,
showing that the light-heavy traffic interpolation recovers the exact
Nelson--Tantawi formula for $n = 2$.
Our $\Tlh$ extends this to the heterogeneous case through the $h(r)$ factor,
while $\Tul$ provides an alternative route via direct bound interpolation.

\subsection{Accuracy and Limitations}

$\Tul$ provides consistently small, positive errors ($\leq 1.2\%$) and is
guaranteed to lie within the bounds.
Its main limitation is that the weight $\alpha = (\rho_1+\rho_2)/8$ is
derived from the homogeneous case and may not optimally represent the
correlation structure at intermediate heterogeneity levels.

$\Tlh$ provides a tighter fit near homogeneity but has larger errors ($\leq 2.4\%$)
at the transition regime ($r \approx 1.5$, heavy load), where the linear
numerator has insufficient flexibility to capture the joint dependence on
both $r$ and $\rho$.
Its parameter $\beta$ has been calibrated to $10$ from available simulation
data; a more systematic calibration over a finer grid of $(r, \rho)$ values
could refine this estimate.
The Reiman--Simon framework also supports a quadratic numerator (matching the
first light-traffic derivative $f'(0)$ as well), which would add one more
degree of freedom and could reduce errors in the transition regime.

\subsection{Recommendations}

For practical use:
\begin{itemize}
\item Use $\Tul$ when a guaranteed bound is required or when high heterogeneity
      ($r \geq 2$) is expected. It is simpler (no free parameters) and
      uniformly accurate.
\item Use $\Tlh$ when theoretical grounding in the Reiman--Simon framework is
      important, or when the system is nearly homogeneous and the tighter
      accuracy at low-to-moderate heterogeneity is valued.
\item Both methods should be used together as a consistency check: agreement
      between $\Tul$ and $\Tlh$ provides confidence in the estimate;
      significant disagreement signals a regime where further analysis
      (e.g., simulation or numerical inversion of the Flatto--Hahn generating
      function) is warranted.
\end{itemize}
