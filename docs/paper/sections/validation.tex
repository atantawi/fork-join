\section{Simulation Validation}
\label{sec:validation}

All three approximations were validated against discrete-event simulation of the
two-queue fork-join system.
Each scenario used 2\,M jobs with a 100\,K-job warmup period and a fixed
random seed for reproducibility.
Results for $\Tul$~\eqref{eq:tul} and $\Tlh$~\eqref{eq:tlh} (with $\beta = 10$)
are shown in Table~\ref{tab:results} alongside the theoretical bounds.
Table~\ref{tab:results-lhe} compares $\Tlh$ and the enhanced approximation
$\Tlhe$~\eqref{eq:tlhe}.

\paragraph{Homogeneous case ($\mu_1 = \mu_2 = 1.0$).}
Both approximations are essentially exact, with errors below $0.1\%$.
The residual error is attributable to finite simulation variance.
This confirms that both methods correctly recover the Nelson--Tantawi
formula~\eqref{eq:nelson-tantawi} by construction.

\paragraph{Moderate heterogeneity ($r = 1.5$).}
$\Tul$ shows consistent positive errors ($+0.67\%$ to $+1.16\%$) across all
loads.
$\Tlh$ is more accurate at low and moderate load, but reaches its largest
error of $+2.40\%$ at heavy load ($\rho_2 = 0.6$).
This worst case for $\Tlh$ arises in the regime where the system is near
the boundary between homogeneous and heterogeneous behavior, where the
linear Reiman--Simon numerator has the least flexibility.

\paragraph{High heterogeneity ($r \geq 2$).}
$\Tul$ errors fall below $0.75\%$ and improve further as $r$ increases
($\leq 0.11\%$ at $r = 5$).
$\Tlh$ errors are slightly larger (up to $+1.80\%$ at $r = 2$) because
the residual correction in $h(r)$ provides a small but non-negligible
contribution even at these heterogeneity levels.
As $r \to \infty$, both approximations converge to $\Tbot$.

Figure~\ref{fig:vs-load} plots the two approximations, bounds, and simulation
as functions of load for $\mu_1 = 1.0$, $\mu_2 = 2.0$.
Both $\Tul$ and $\Tlh$ track the simulation closely across the full load
range, lying between the theoretical bounds.
Figure~\ref{fig:vs-hetero} shows the same quantities as a function of
the heterogeneity ratio $\mu_2/\mu_1$ at fixed $\lambda = 0.6$.
Near homogeneity ($r \approx 1$), $\Tlh$ is more accurate; for $r \geq 3$,
$\Tul$ is slightly tighter.

\paragraph{Homogeneous case ($\mu_1 = \mu_2 = 1.0$).}
As proved in Section~\ref{sec:approx-lhe}, the quadratic coefficient $c_2 = 0$
when $r = 1$, so $\Tlhe \equiv \Tlh$.
Both recover Nelson--Tantawi exactly; residual errors are below $0.1\%$ due
to finite simulation variance.

\paragraph{Moderate heterogeneity ($r = 1.5$).}
$\Tlhe$ offers no improvement here and is slightly less accurate than $\Tlh$
(worst case $+2.77\%$ vs.\ $+2.41\%$ at heavy load).
This regime is precisely where the power-law model $h(r) = 1 + \tfrac{3}{8}r^{-\beta}$
is least reliable: the true heavy-traffic constant $h$ varies sharply near $r = 1$,
and the $\beta$-approximation introduces systematic error that the additional
derivative term cannot correct.

\paragraph{High heterogeneity ($r \geq 2$).}
$\Tlhe$ consistently outperforms $\Tlh$:
at $r = 2$ and $\lambda \in \{0.3, 0.6\}$ errors fall from $+0.87\%,\,+1.70\%$
to $+0.45\%,\,+1.20\%$; at $r = 3$ from $+1.45\%$ to $+0.57\%$;
and at $r = 5$ from $+0.75\%$ to $+0.19\%$.
In this regime $h(r) \approx 1$ and the first derivative $f^{(1)}(0)$ carries
meaningful information about the service-time heterogeneity that improves the
approximation over a wide load range.
